<p style="font-size: 1.3rem; line-height: 1.6; color: #0a192f; font-weight: 300; border-left: 4px solid #d4af37; padding: 6px 0 6px 22px; margin: 10px 0 40px 0;">
I design <strong>defect-engineered perovskite electrocatalysts</strong> and translate them into <strong>industrial-scale green-hydrogen electrolyzers</strong> — bridging atomic-scale materials science with the deployment realities of the energy transition.
</p>

<p style="font-size: 1.05rem; line-height: 1.8;">
The decarbonisation of heavy industry depends on <strong>green hydrogen</strong> produced by water electrolysis powered by renewable electricity. Today that hydrogen is too expensive, largely because the electrocatalysts driving the oxygen and hydrogen evolution reactions rely on scarce precious metals and lose activity at industrial scale. My research attacks this bottleneck from both ends at once — the atomic scale where catalytic activity is born, and the plant floor where it must survive. The framework below follows the three axes on which such work is evaluated: <strong>Excellence, Impact, and Implementation</strong>.
</p>

<h2 style="color:#0a192f; border-bottom:2px solid #d4af37; padding-bottom:8px; margin-top:45px;">01 · Excellence — the science</h2>

<p style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.8;">
The perovskite oxide family (ABO₃) is uniquely tunable: substitute the A- or B-site cation, or engineer oxygen vacancies, and its catalytic behaviour shifts predictably. My doctoral and postdoctoral work established that <strong>imperfection is a design tool</strong> — that deliberately introduced defects can out-perform the pristine lattice. Highlights of this contribution:
</p>

<ul style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.9;">
<li><strong>Defect engineering in K-substituted LaMnO₃</strong> — a bifunctional oxygen catalyst stable beyond 1000 charge–discharge cycles in zinc–air batteries (first/corresponding author, <em>ACS Appl. Energy Mater.</em>).</li>
<li><strong>Spin-selective catalysis</strong> — chiral Ni–Au systems that exploit the CISS effect to raise hydrogen-evolution efficiency by ~10%, opening a physics-driven route to more efficient electrolysis (Weizmann Institute).</li>
<li><strong>Low-temperature synthesis of metastable phases</strong> — hydrothermal routes at 200–240 °C that stabilise perovskite phases normally requiring &gt;1000 °C calcination, preserving the nanoscale surface area that catalysis demands.</li>
</ul>

<p style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.8;">
This body of work spans <strong>11 peer-reviewed publications</strong> in journals including <em>J. Mater. Chem. A</em>, <em>ACS Applied Energy Materials</em>, and <em>Nanoscale</em>, supported by deep hands-on mastery of electron microscopy (FEG-TEM), X-ray diffraction with Rietveld refinement, XPS, and the full suite of electrochemical methods.
</p>

<h2 style="color:#0a192f; border-bottom:2px solid #d4af37; padding-bottom:8px; margin-top:45px;">02 · Impact — why it matters</h2>

<p style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.8;">
Green hydrogen sits at the centre of the <strong>EU Green Deal and REPowerEU</strong> agendas and of India's National Green Hydrogen Mission. The scientific barrier to both is the same: electrolysers that are cheap, durable, and built from earth-abundant elements. My current role as <strong>Scientist-B at BPCL Corporate R&D (CRDC)</strong> places that barrier directly in front of me:
</p>

<ul style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.9;">
<li>Leading development of a <strong>commercial-scale alkaline water electrolyser</strong> — advancing OER/HER and AOR/HER electrocatalyst systems from milligram discovery toward industrial deployment.</li>
<li>Exploring <strong>electrochemical H₂S valorisation</strong> — turning a refinery waste stream into a lower-energy anodic alternative to oxygen evolution.</li>
<li>Developing <strong>retardant catalyst materials for a ceramic hydrogen burner</strong> — closing the loop on safe hydrogen combustion.</li>
</ul>

<p style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.8;">
Few materials scientists work simultaneously at the fundamental and the industrial ends of this problem. That dual vantage — <strong>knowing which laboratory results actually survive scale-up</strong> — is the impact I bring to any collaboration or fellowship.
</p>

<h2 style="color:#0a192f; border-bottom:2px solid #d4af37; padding-bottom:8px; margin-top:45px;">03 · Implementation — how I deliver it</h2>

<p style="font-size:1.05rem; line-height:1.8;">
My method is a repeatable pipeline from atom to device: a <strong>soft-chemistry synthesis platform</strong> that tunes defects by design, a <strong>full characterisation stack</strong> that verifies structure–property links, and an <strong>industrial validation loop</strong> that tests candidates against real feedstocks and duty cycles. Each stage feeds the next, so promising chemistry is filtered early for scalability rather than late.
</p>
